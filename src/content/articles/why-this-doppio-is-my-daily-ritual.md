<p>Some mornings, the first sip of coffee isn't just about waking up. It's a quiet anchor. For me, that anchor is a doppio. Not a latte, not a pour-over—just two ounces of espresso, pulled with intention. The ritual is what makes it work. The sound of the grinder, the tamp, the hiss of water through the puck. It takes less than two minutes, but it resets my focus.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/13528195-7740-472b-9564-ea64780f48f6/0_3.png" alt="Close-up of ground coffee in a portafilter after tamping, showing a level surface" /></figure>


<h2>Why a Doppio, Not a Single Shot</h2>
<p>A doppio is a double espresso. But calling it just "double" undersells it. A single shot can be thin and fleeting. A doppio has weight. The ratio—18 grams of coffee to 36 grams of water—gives you a shot that's balanced. It's not a jolt of bitterness. It's a smooth, layered sip that holds sweetness and acidity in check. I keep the ratio at exactly 1:2. That's the sweet spot for most medium roasts. It pulls the sugars and oils without crossing into harsh territory.</p>

<h2>The Specifics That Make It Work</h2>
<p>Here's the part that matters: the numbers. I use 18 grams of coffee, ground fine enough to feel like table salt. Not powdered, not gritty. The water temperature is 93°C. If your machine doesn't show temperature, run a blank shot through the group head first. That warms everything up. The extraction should take 28 seconds. Not 20, not 35. If the first drops appear after 7 seconds, you're in the ballpark. The stream should look like warm honey—steady, not sputtering. If it's too fast, your grind is too coarse. If it chokes, grind finer.</p>
<p>One mistake I see often: tamping too hard or unevenly. A level 30-pound tamp is enough. You don't need to break the table. After tamping, the puck should be smooth and level. Any tilt will channel the water through one side, leaving the rest dry. That's how you get sour and bitter in the same sip.</p>

<h2>The Crema Tells the Story</h2>
<p>Crema isn't just decoration. It's the emulsion of oils and carbon dioxide. A good crema should be thick, golden, and persistent. It holds the aromatics. When I swirl the shot, the crema blends into the liquid, softening the acidity. If the crema is thin and white, the beans are old or the extraction is off. If it's dark with blotches, the shot is over-extracted. That's a sign to adjust your grind or temperature.</p>

<h2>How It Fits Into My Day</h2>
<p>This doppio is my daily ritual because it's repeatable. I don't chase novelty. I chase consistency. Once I dial in a bean—usually a medium roast with chocolate and stone fruit notes—I lock those settings. Every morning, I weigh the beans, grind, tamp, pull. The whole process is quiet. No phone. No rush. Just the sound of water and the smell of fresh grounds. It's a small pause before the day takes over.</p>
<p>Caffeine affects people differently. Some can drink a doppio at 8 p.m. and sleep fine. Others feel jittery after half a cup. Pay attention to how your body responds. If you're sensitive, consider a half-caff blend or a single shot. And if you have a health condition or are pregnant, talk to a doctor about your caffeine intake. Coffee isn't for young children, either.</p>

<h2>The Takeaway</h2>
<p>A doppio doesn't have to be complicated. But it does need care. Use a scale. Control your water temperature. Watch the timing. The reward is a shot that's sweet, balanced, and energizing—not a bitter chore. That's why this doppio is my daily ritual. It's not about the caffeine. It's about the two minutes I take to do something right.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>What is a doppio?</h3>
<p>A doppio is simply a double espresso shot. It uses about 18 grams of coffee and yields around 36 grams of liquid, following a 1:2 ratio. It's the standard base for many espresso drinks.</p>
<h3>Why 93°C for the water temperature?</h3>
<p>93°C is a good starting point for most medium roasts. It extracts the bright acids and sugars without pulling too much bitterness. If your coffee tastes sour, try a slightly higher temperature. If it's bitter, lower it a degree.</p>
<h3>How do I know if my doppio is balanced?</h3>
<p>A balanced doppio has a sweetness that carries through, with a mild acidity and no harsh bitterness. The crema should be thick and persistent. If it's sour, the shot may be under-extracted. If it's dry or ashy, it's over-extracted.</p>
<h3>Can I make a doppio without an espresso machine?</h3>
<p>You can approximate a concentrated coffee using a Moka pot or an Aeropress with a fine grind, but the pressure and crema won't match a true espresso machine. For a proper doppio, an espresso machine is needed.</p>
