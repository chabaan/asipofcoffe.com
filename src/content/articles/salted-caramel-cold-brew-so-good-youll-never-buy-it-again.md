<p>The first time I made salted caramel cold brew at home, I was skeptical. Store-bought versions always hit that sweet-salty note, but they also come with a list of ingredients I can't pronounce and a price tag that makes me wince. So I set out to crack the code: a cold brew that's smooth, not bitter, and a caramel that actually stays liquid when it hits ice—no corn syrup required.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/91198bc8-ac69-4872-9eb2-2ccd94f1eb93/0_2.png" alt="Pouring cold brew coffee through a cheesecloth-lined strainer into a glass jar" /></figure>
<p>Here's the thing: most homemade versions fail because the caramel seizes up or the coffee turns harsh. I spent a few batches figuring out why. The answer is technique, not fancy equipment. Let me walk you through it.</p><h2>The Cold Brew Base: Why Ratio Matters</h2><p>Cold brew is forgiving—until it isn't. The magic number for a concentrate that's bold but not aggressive is <strong>1:4 coffee to water by weight</strong>. That's 100 grams of coffee to 400 grams (about 4 cups) of water. Use a coarse grind, like breadcrumbs. Too fine and you'll get silt and bitterness. Too coarse and it'll be weak.</p><p>I steep mine for exactly 18 hours. Less than 12 and it's under-extracted—thin and sour. More than 24 and it pulls out the harsh, woody notes. Set a timer on your phone. Really.</p><p>When you strain, let it drip through a cheesecloth-lined sieve. Never press or squeeze the grounds. That forces out bitter compounds that would otherwise stay trapped. You'll get a clean, silky concentrate that tastes almost sweet on its own.</p><h2>The Caramel: A 5-Minute Game Changer</h2><p>This is where most homemade versions go wrong. They use a wet caramel (sugar plus water) but don't cook it far enough, so it's thin and grainy. Or they add butter, which separates when it hits cold coffee. I use heavy cream because it emulsifies smoothly and stays pourable even after refrigeration.</p><p>Start with 1/2 cup sugar and 2 tablespoons water in a small saucepan. Swirl it gently over medium heat—don't stir, or you'll get crystals. Watch for the color to shift from clear to pale yellow to deep amber. The moment it looks like a new penny, pull it off the heat. That's about 5-7 minutes. If you wait another 30 seconds, it'll burn and taste acrid.</p><p>Add the cream carefully—it will bubble up violently. Whisk until smooth. Then stir in flaky salt. Let it cool for 5 minutes. It will thicken to a syrupy consistency. If it gets too thick, add a teaspoon of hot water and whisk.</p><p>A common mistake: adding salt too early. Salt can cause the sugar to seize if added while the caramel is still bubbling. Always add it after you've whisked in the cream and removed it from heat.</p><h2>Assembling the Drink</h2><p>Fill a glass with ice. Pour 1/2 cup cold brew concentrate over it. Add 1/2 cup cold water or milk—I use whole milk for body, but oat milk works well too. Drizzle 1-2 tablespoons of caramel over the top. It'll sink and swirl on its own. Finish with a pinch of flaky salt right before serving.</p><p>Don't stir immediately. Let the caramel settle for a few seconds, then take a sip from the top—you'll get the coffee first, then a wave of salted sweetness. Stir it in when you're ready for a more uniform drink.</p><h2>Why This Works Better Than Store-Bought</h2><p>Store-bought salted caramel cold brews rely on artificial flavors and stabilizers to keep the caramel from separating. This version uses real caramel, which naturally emulsifies with the cream. The cold brew itself is smoother because you control the steep time. And the salt? Flaky salt gives a crunch that dissolved salt just can't match.</p><p>One more thing: this caramel isn't just for coffee. I've used it on pancakes, ice cream, and even stirred into oatmeal. It keeps in the fridge for two weeks—just warm it slightly before using.</p><h2>A Quick Note on Caffeine</h2><p>Cold brew concentrate is typically more caffeinated per ounce than hot coffee. If you're sensitive to caffeine, start with a smaller amount (1/4 cup) and dilute more. Caffeine affects everyone differently, so listen to your body. For those with specific health concerns or during pregnancy, it's always wise to check with a qualified professional. Coffee is not recommended for young children.</p><p>Now, go make that cold brew. You'll never look at a plastic bottle the same way again.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Why is my cold brew bitter?</h3>
<p>You likely steeped too long (over 24 hours) or used too fine a grind. Stick to coarse grounds and 12-24 hours. Also, don't press the grounds when straining—that releases bitter compounds.</p>
<h3>Can I use store-bought caramel sauce?</h3>
<p>You can, but most have preservatives and corn syrup that taste flat. This homemade version takes 5 minutes and has a cleaner, deeper flavor that actually complements the coffee.</p>
<h3>How strong is this cold brew concentrate?</h3>
<p>The 1:4 ratio (coffee to water) yields a concentrate about 2x stronger than regular drip coffee. I dilute it 1:1 with water or milk. Adjust to your preference.</p>
<h3>Is this drink safe during pregnancy?</h3>
<p>Caffeine affects everyone differently. Cold brew is often higher in caffeine per ounce. If you are pregnant or nursing, consult your healthcare provider about safe caffeine limits. Coffee is not suitable for young children.</p>
