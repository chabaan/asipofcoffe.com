<p>The first sip of a well-made cortadito is a quiet surprise. It's not the loud sweetness of a latte or the sharp jolt of a straight espresso. It's something in between—a small, balanced drink where the coffee still leads, but the milk softens the edges without washing them away. And when you get the sugar right, it feels like a treat you didn't have to leave home for.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/3c88ffe5-9168-4cf7-855e-de53463688b1/0_3.png" alt="Steaming milk for a cortadito with a thermometer showing 65°C" /></figure>
<h2>What Makes a Cortadito a Cortadito</h2><p>At its core, a cortadito is a Cuban-inspired espresso drink. The name means 'little cut,' referring to the way milk cuts through the coffee. But unlike a macchiato, which is mostly espresso with a dollop of milk, or a cortado, which uses unsweetened espresso, the cortadito relies on sugar—specifically demerara or turbinado—to build body and depth. The sugar is not an afterthought. It's stirred directly into the hot espresso until it dissolves into a thick syrup, creating a base that carries the milk rather than just floating on top.</p><p>The ratio I keep is simple: one double shot of espresso (18 grams of coffee in, 36 grams out) to about 60 milliliters of steamed milk. That's roughly 1:1 by volume, though I adjust by a few milliliters depending on the milk's fat content. Whole milk gives a creamier mouthfeel; oat milk (barista blend) works too, but it needs a slightly lower temperature to avoid scorching.</p><h2>The Sugar: Timing and Type Matter</h2><p>Use demerara or turbinado sugar—the coarse, amber crystals that resist clumping and dissolve slowly. White granulated sugar works in a pinch but lacks the subtle molasses note that rounds out the coffee's acidity. Add the sugar to the empty demitasse cup before brewing the espresso, or pour the shot directly over the sugar and stir immediately. The key is to dissolve it fully while the espresso is still above 80°C. If you wait, the sugar won't integrate and you'll get a gritty first sip and a syrupy last one.</p><p>One tip I've found useful: add a tiny pinch of salt (just a few grains) to the sugar before stirring. It doesn't make the drink salty—it reduces bitterness and lifts the sweetness, making the coffee taste more rounded. This is not a common step in most recipes, but it's the kind of adjustment that separates a good cortadito from a great one.</p><h2>Pulling the Espresso: Precision Over Speed</h2><p>For the cortadito, the espresso needs to be balanced. Not too bright, not too dark. I aim for a brew ratio of 1:2 (18g in, 36g out) in 27-30 seconds. If the shot runs faster than 25 seconds, it will taste sour against the sugar. If it runs longer than 32 seconds, it turns bitter and the milk can't rescue it. The crema should be a deep hazel color with a slight tiger-stripe pattern—that's a sign the extraction is even.</p><p>If you're using a moka pot, grind slightly coarser than espresso (think fine sand) and use low heat. Brew until the coffee begins to sputter, then remove from heat immediately to avoid burning the grounds. The resulting concentrate will be less viscous than espresso, so reduce the milk to 50 milliliters to maintain the 1:1 balance.</p><h2>Milk Texture: Less Is More</h2><p>The milk for a cortadito should be steamed to 60-65°C (140-150°F). Any hotter and the milk proteins break down, creating a thin, watery texture and a scalded flavor. I steam with the wand tip just below the surface until the milk doubles in volume, then submerge it to heat through. The goal is a thin layer of microfoam—about 1 centimeter—not the thick blanket you'd use on a cappuccino. When you pour, hold back the foam with a spoon and let the milk flow into the sweetened espresso, then spoon a small cap of foam on top.</p><p>Common mistake: over-aerating the milk. Too much foam turns the cortadito into a mini cappuccino, diluting the coffee's presence. A cortadito should still taste primarily of espresso, with the milk as a supporting note.</p><h2>The Pour and Final Touch</h2><p>Pour the milk slowly into the center of the cup, allowing the espresso and sugar syrup to rise and mix naturally. Don't stir after pouring—the milk should integrate on its own. The finished drink should be about 120 milliliters total, served in a 4-ounce demitasse cup. The surface should have a single, uninterrupted circle of foam, like a small moon on the dark coffee.</p><p>I drink it immediately, while the foam is still firm and the temperature is around 55°C. It cools quickly, so there's no time to linger. That's part of the ritual: a small, focused moment with a cup that asks for nothing more than your full attention.</p><h2>Takeaway</h2><p>A cortadito that feels like a treat doesn't require rare beans or expensive equipment. It needs a precise ratio, the right sugar, and a careful hand with the milk. Once you dial in those three elements, you can recreate that small pleasure any morning—without leaving your kitchen. Remember, caffeine affects everyone differently; start with one serving if you're sensitive, and consult a healthcare professional for personal dietary advice.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>What is the difference between a cortadito and a cortado?</h3>
<p>A cortadito is a Cuban-style espresso drink sweetened with demerara sugar before adding milk, while a cortado is a Spanish-style drink with unsweetened espresso and a equal part of steamed milk. The cortadito's sugar is integral to its flavor and texture.</p>
<h3>Can I make a cortadito without an espresso machine?</h3>
<p>Yes, you can use a moka pot to brew strong coffee (aim for a 1:3 coffee-to-water ratio) and then sweeten it with demerara sugar. Heat milk separately and whisk for froth, then combine in a 1:1 ratio.</p>
<h3>Why add sugar before milk in a cortadito?</h3>
<p>Adding sugar to hot espresso dissolves it fully and creates a syrup-like base that integrates evenly with the milk. This ensures every sip is consistently sweet without gritty crystals.</p>
<h3>What milk works best for a cortadito?</h3>
<p>Whole milk provides the richest texture and microfoam. Oat milk (barista blend) is a good dairy-free alternative, but avoid skim milk as it produces thin foam that collapses quickly.</p>
<h3>Is a cortadito high in caffeine?</h3>
<p>A cortadito contains roughly 60-100 mg of caffeine per serving, similar to a standard double espresso. Caffeine sensitivity varies, so start with a smaller amount if you are not accustomed to espresso. Consult a doctor for personal health advice.</p>
