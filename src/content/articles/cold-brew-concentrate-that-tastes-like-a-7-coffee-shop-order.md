<p>I remember the first time I paid seven dollars for a cold brew. The barista poured a dark, silky stream over ice, and one sip told me why it cost that much. No bitterness. No sharp edges. Just a smooth, chocolatey depth that lingered. I knew I could reverse-engineer that concentrate at home — and I did.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/6cff757c-4a7d-4ff8-9c1d-ba574fbf7130/0_2.png" alt="Coarse coffee grounds in a glass jar with water being stirred with a long spoon" /></figure>


<h2>The Ratio That Makes the Difference</h2>
<p>The secret to a $7 cold brew isn't a secret at all. It's the ratio. Most home recipes use a 1:5 or 1:6 coffee-to-water ratio by volume, which gives a weak brew that needs to be drunk straight. That's not a concentrate. A true concentrate — the kind shops use — is 1:4 by weight. That means 100 grams of coffee to 400 grams of water. I use a kitchen scale every time. Volume measurements are too inconsistent; a cup of beans can vary by 10 grams depending on how they settle.</p>
<p>Why 1:4? At this ratio, the coffee grounds are fully submerged, and the extraction hits a sweet spot. You get enough soluble solids for a rich body, but not so much that the bitter compounds dominate. The result is a concentrate that dilutes beautifully without losing its character.</p>

<h2>Grind Size: Coarse, Not Careless</h2>
<p>I grind my beans to a coarse consistency — think sea salt or breadcrumbs. If you're using a blade grinder, pulse in short bursts to avoid creating fines (the powdery bits that cause over-extraction and muddiness). A burr grinder is better for consistency, but either works if you're careful.</p>
<p>Why coarse? Cold water extracts slowly. Fine grounds release tannins and bitter compounds too quickly, even at room temperature. Coarse grounds give a clean, smooth extraction over 12 hours. The visual cue: after steeping, the grounds should look like wet sand, not sludge. If they're muddy, your grind was too fine.</p>

<h2>The 12-Hour Room-Temperature Steep</h2>
<p>Here's where most home recipes go wrong. They tell you to steep in the fridge for 24 hours. That's a mistake. Cold water slows extraction, and 24 hours in the fridge often yields a weak, slightly sour brew because the water never gets warm enough to dissolve the full range of flavors. Room temperature — around 68-72°F (20-22°C) — is ideal. At this temperature, extraction happens steadily over 12 hours. You get the full spectrum of oils and solids without the heat that would release bitterness.</p>
<p>I start my steep in the evening and strain it the next morning. The visual cue: after 12 hours, the liquid should be deep brown-black, almost opaque. If it looks thin or tea-like, give it another 2 hours. But don't go past 14 hours without tasting — over-steeping can turn it harsh.</p>

<h2>Straining: Handle With Care</h2>
<p>When the steep is done, I line a fine-mesh strainer with a damp paper coffee filter (or cheesecloth) and pour the mixture through. The key: let it drip naturally. Do not press or squeeze the grounds. Squeezing forces out bitter compounds that the water didn't extract. I let it drain for about 10 minutes. For an extra-clean concentrate — the kind that leaves no sediment in your glass — I strain it a second time through a fresh filter.</p>
<p>The visual cue: the finished concentrate should be perfectly clear, with no cloudiness or grit. If it's cloudy, your grind was too fine or you squeezed the grounds.</p>

<h2>Dilution: The Final Touch</h2>
<p>Now for the part that makes it taste like a $7 order. I dilute 1 part concentrate with 1 part cold water or milk. For a stronger cup, use a 2:1 ratio (two parts concentrate to one part milk). I pour it over a full glass of ice — large cubes melt slower and keep the drink concentrated. The first sip should be smooth, with a cocoa-like finish and zero bitterness. If it's too strong, add a splash more water. Too weak? Next time, steep for 13 hours or increase the coffee to 110 grams.</p>

<h2>A Note on Caffeine</h2>
<p>Cold brew concentrate is more concentrated, meaning it contains more caffeine per ounce than regular drip coffee. If you're sensitive to caffeine or have a health condition, start with a smaller serving (1 ounce of concentrate) and see how you feel. Coffee and caffeine are not suitable for young children. For any personal health decisions, consult a qualified professional.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>The difference between a $7 cold brew and a home version is precision. Use a 1:4 ratio by weight, a coarse grind, a 12-hour room-temperature steep, and a gentle strain. That's it. No specialty equipment, no fancy beans. Just methodical technique that delivers every time.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Why should I steep at room temperature instead of in the fridge?</h3>
<p>Room temperature allows for a more even and efficient extraction over 12 hours. Refrigeration slows the process, often leading to under-extraction and a weaker, more acidic concentrate. The 12-hour room-temp steep is the standard for the smooth, full-bodied concentrate you get at shops.</p>
<h3>Can I use any coffee bean for this recipe?</h3>
<p>Yes, but medium roasts are ideal. Dark roasts can turn bitter when concentrated, and light roasts may taste too bright or acidic. A good medium roast gives that classic chocolatey, low-acid profile. Experiment with single origins if you want, but start with a balanced blend.</p>
<h3>How long does the concentrate last in the fridge?</h3>
<p>Up to 2 weeks in a sealed container. After that, oxidation can dull the flavor. If you notice a sour or off taste, it's time to brew a fresh batch.</p>
<h3>What's the best coffee-to-water ratio for concentrate?</h3>
<p>By weight, 1 part coffee to 4 parts water (e.g., 100g coffee to 400g water). This yields a concentrate that dilutes 1:1 with water or milk. If you prefer a stronger concentrate (for smaller doses), use 1:3, but adjust steeping time to 10 hours to avoid bitterness.</p>
