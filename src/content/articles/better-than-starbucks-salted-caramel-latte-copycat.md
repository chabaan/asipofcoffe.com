<p>The first time I tried a salted caramel latte from a certain green-apron chain, I was excited. Then I took a sip. It was sweet, sure, but the coffee was buried under a sugary syrup that tasted more artificial than caramel. The salt? Barely there. I knew I could make a better version at home—one where the coffee actually shines, the caramel is real, and the salt balances rather than overwhelms.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/dbe64bb7-77ea-4940-a44e-77be300f4ada/0_3.png" alt="Saucepan with amber caramel bubbling on the stove" /></figure>
<p>This recipe is that version. It uses a precise espresso-to-milk ratio (1:3), a quick homemade caramel with just sugar and water, and flaky sea salt that you can taste. No corn syrup, no preservatives, no guessing. Let's get to it.</p><h2>The Secret: Real Caramel, Not Syrup</h2><p>Most copycat recipes rely on caramel syrup from a bottle. That's fine, but it's also why the chain version tastes flat. Real caramel is made by heating sugar until it turns amber—a process called caramelization. That gives you complex notes of toffee, butter, and a hint of bitterness that cuts through the milk.</p><p><strong>Key technique:</strong> Use a clean, dry saucepan. Combine 2 tablespoons of granulated sugar with 1 tablespoon of water. Swirl the pan gently—do <em>not</em> stir with a spoon, or you'll introduce crystals that make the caramel grainy. Watch carefully: the sugar will bubble and darken. When it reaches a deep amber (think the color of a penny), remove it from the heat. Immediately add 2 tablespoons of milk—it will steam and sputter, so keep your hands back. Stir until smooth. That's it. No butter, no cream, no fuss.</p><h2>The Ratio That Makes It a Latte</h2><p>A latte is defined by its milk-to-espresso ratio. For a balanced drink that isn't too milky or too strong, I keep the ratio at <strong>1 part espresso to 3 parts milk</strong>. For a standard 12-ounce drink, that means 2 ounces of espresso (two shots) and 6 ounces of frothed milk. The caramel adds sweetness, so I don't adjust the ratio for that.</p><p><strong>Common mistake:</strong> Using too much milk. If you fill the cup to the brim, you'll dilute the coffee flavor. Measure your milk before frothing. Also, don't overheat the milk—aim for 150°F (65°C). Above 160°F, it starts to scorch and lose sweetness.</p><h2>Brewing the Espresso</h2><p>I use a standard espresso machine with a 1:2 brew ratio: 18 grams of coffee in, 36 grams of espresso out, in about 25-30 seconds. If you don't have an espresso machine, a Moka pot works beautifully. Use a medium-fine grind (like table salt) and fill the basket without tamping. Brew until the top chamber is full—that gives you a concentrated, crema-topped base.</p><p>For a stronger coffee flavor, try a lungo shot (1:3 ratio) or use a dark roast bean. The caramel handles bitterness well, so don't be afraid of a bold roast.</p><h2>Assembling the Drink</h2><p>Drizzle about half your caramel into the bottom of a warm glass or mug. Pour the hot espresso over it—this helps melt the caramel into the coffee. Then slowly add the frothed milk, holding back the foam with a spoon so the milk pours first. Top with the remaining foam. Finish with a drizzle of caramel and a generous pinch of flaky sea salt. The salt should be visible on top, not dissolved.</p><p><strong>Texture cue:</strong> The frothed milk should look like wet paint—smooth, with tiny bubbles. If you see large bubbles, tap the pitcher on the counter to break them, or swirl the milk gently.</p><h2>Making It Iced</h2><p>For an iced version, let the caramel cool to room temperature. Fill a glass with ice, pour in the caramel, then add cold milk (no need to froth). Brew the espresso and let it cool for 30 seconds before pouring over the ice. This prevents the ice from melting too quickly. Stir gently, then top with a pinch of salt.</p><h2>A Note on Caffeine</h2><p>Caffeine affects people differently. If you're sensitive or have a health condition, consider using decaf or a half-caff blend. Consult a qualified professional for personal advice. This drink is not suitable for young children.</p><h2>Takeaway</h2><p>This salted caramel latte is better than the chain version because it respects the coffee. The caramel is real, the salt is present, and the ratio is precise. Once you've made it your way, you'll wonder why you ever waited in line.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I use store-bought caramel sauce instead?</h3>
<p>Yes, but homemade caramel gives a cleaner, less cloying sweetness. If using store-bought, choose one without corn syrup and add a pinch of sea salt.</p>
<h3>What if I don't have an espresso machine?</h3>
<p>Brew a very strong cup of coffee using a Moka pot or a French press with a 1:10 coffee-to-water ratio. Aim for about 4 ounces of concentrated coffee.</p>
<h3>How do I make this dairy-free?</h3>
<p>Use oat milk for the creamiest texture. Almond or coconut milk will work but may separate slightly.</p>
<h3>Why does my caramel turn grainy?</h3>
<p>Crystals form if you stir too early or the pan is not clean. Swirl, don't stir, and use a clean pan with a pinch of corn syrup or lemon juice to prevent crystallization.</p>
