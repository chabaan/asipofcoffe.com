<p>There’s a particular stillness to a hot afternoon—the air thick, the sun relentless. What cuts through it is a glass of cold brew, dark and smooth, with just enough sweetness to feel like a treat. I’ve made cold brew countless ways, but this brown sugar version is the one I come back to when the heat demands something effortless.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/6131ca28-73ef-43fb-a248-d8fef708aa2b/0_3.png" alt="Pouring cold brew through a cheesecloth-lined strainer into a glass bowl" /></figure>


<h2>Why Cold Brew Works for Hot Days</h2>
<p>Cold brew isn’t just iced coffee made with cold water. The long, cool extraction pulls out the coffee’s natural sweetness and low-acid body. The result is a concentrate that stays smooth even when diluted. No bitterness, no sharp edges. That’s why it’s my go-to for hot days—it’s forgiving, hands-off, and rewards patience with a clean finish.</p>

<h2>The Ratio That Works Every Time</h2>
<p>I keep the ratio at <strong>1 part coffee to 4 parts water</strong> by weight. That’s 100 grams of coarsely ground coffee to 400 grams (or 400 ml) of water. This yields a concentrate that’s strong enough to hold up to milk or water without tasting thin. If you prefer a more intense brew, shift to 1:3. For a lighter version, 1:5. But 1:4 is the sweet spot—balanced, versatile, and forgiving of minor measurement errors.</p>

<h2>Grind Size: The Most Common Mistake</h2>
<p>Grind for cold brew like you’re making coarse breadcrumbs. A burr grinder set to the coarsest setting works best. Too fine, and you’ll over-extract during the long steep—resulting in a muddy, bitter concentrate. Too coarse, and you’ll under-extract, leaving you with weak, sour brew. I check my grind by pinching a few grounds: they should feel rough, not powdery. If you see dust, go coarser.</p>

<h2>Step-by-Step: Brown Sugar Cold Brew</h2>
<h3>1. Combine Coffee and Water</h3>
<p>In a clean 1-quart mason jar or pitcher, add the coarsely ground coffee. Pour the cold filtered water over the grounds. Stir gently with a spoon until all the grounds are wet. No need to stir again during steeping—agitation can introduce bitterness.</p>

<h3>2. Steep at Room Temperature</h3>
<p>Cover the jar and let it sit at room temperature for exactly 12 hours. I set a timer on my phone. Room temperature (around 68–72°F or 20–22°C) is ideal. A fridge steep will take longer and extract less. A warmer kitchen will speed extraction but risk over-extraction. Twelve hours at room temp is the sweet spot.</p>

<h3>3. Strain Without Squeezing</h3>
<p>Line a fine-mesh strainer with a dampened cheesecloth or a paper coffee filter. Place it over a clean bowl or pitcher. Slowly pour the cold brew mixture through. Let it drain naturally—do not press or squeeze the grounds. That forces bitter compounds into the brew. The final concentrate should be clear, with a deep brown color and a silky mouthfeel.</p>

<h3>4. Add Brown Sugar</h3>
<p>While the concentrate is still cool (but not ice-cold), stir in the brown sugar. I use 3 tablespoons (40g) packed brown sugar for this batch. The sugar dissolves more easily at room temperature than in a fridge-cold liquid. Stir until no grains remain. Taste and adjust: a little more sugar if you want a dessert-like sweetness, less if you prefer the coffee forward. Dark brown sugar adds a deeper molasses note; light brown is milder.</p>

<h2>How to Serve</h2>
<p>Fill a tall glass with ice. Pour 1 part concentrate to 1 part cold water or milk. Stir. The brown sugar will have already dissolved, so no gritty residue. I like whole milk for a creamy body, but oat milk’s subtle sweetness pairs beautifully with the brown sugar. For a black version, use water and a dash of salt to brighten the flavors.</p>

<h2>The One Tip Most People Miss</h2>
<p>Here’s the detail that sets this method apart: <strong>add the brown sugar after steeping, not before.</strong> Sugar in the steep can slow extraction and mess with the coffee’s chemistry. By adding it to the finished concentrate, you keep the brew clean and the sweetness precise. You also avoid the risk of fermentation if the sugar sits too long at room temperature.</p>

<h2>Storage and Shelf Life</h2>
<p>Store the concentrate in a sealed container in the fridge. It stays fresh for up to 2 weeks. The brown sugar will not ferment or sour if kept cold. If you see any off smells or mold, discard it. I usually make a batch every Sunday and use it through the week.</p>

<h2>A Note on Caffeine</h2>
<p>Cold brew concentrate is more concentrated than drip coffee—one serving can have 150–200 mg of caffeine, depending on the ratio. Caffeine affects everyone differently. If you’re sensitive, try a smaller serving or dilute with more water or milk. For those managing health conditions or pregnancy, it’s wise to consult a qualified professional. Coffee is not suitable for young children.</p>

<p><strong>Takeaway:</strong> This brown sugar cold brew is the easiest way to beat the heat. Coarse grind, 12-hour steep, sugar after. That’s it. A glass of this on a hot day is all the reward you need.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I use white sugar instead of brown sugar?</h3>
<p>Yes, but brown sugar adds a deeper, molasses-like sweetness that complements the cold brew's smoothness. If you use white sugar, add it to the concentrate while it's still cool and stir until dissolved.</p>
<h3>How long does brown sugar cold brew last in the fridge?</h3>
<p>Stored in an airtight container, the concentrate stays fresh for up to 2 weeks. The brown sugar will not ferment if kept cold.</p>
<h3>What grind size should I use for cold brew?</h3>
<p>Use a coarse grind, similar to breadcrumbs. A fine grind will over-extract and create bitterness. A medium-coarse is acceptable if you don't have a burr grinder.</p>
<h3>Can I make this without a scale?</h3>
<p>Yes, but a scale ensures consistency. Without one, use 1 cup of whole beans (coarsely ground) per 4 cups of water. Adjust to taste.</p>
